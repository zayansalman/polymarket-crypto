## What Zayan described

- Read the 1h direction as a probability and estimate volatility.
- 100% up and zero volatility: 4 Up in the 15m market. 80% up and zero volatility: 4 Up and 1 Down.
  High volatility: more Up/Down mixing, still weighted toward the 1h direction.
- In his words, a smarter random bet, or a smarter dice roll.

Two readings were researched: (a) split the stake across Up and Down in the same window in
proportion to the probability; (b) roll a weighted die each window and bet one side.

## How each market settles (checked 2026-09-22)

| market | settles on | rule |
|---|---|---|
| BTC 5m, 15m | Chainlink BTC/USD 60 s TWAP stream | Up if the TWAP print at the window close is >= the print at the open (`priceToBeat`) |
| BTC 1h | Binance BTC/USDT 1h candle | Up if close >= open |
| BTC 24h | Binance BTC/USDT 1m candles | Up if the 12:00 ET 1m close on day 2 is above the 12:00 ET 1m close on day 1; equal resolves 50-50 |

The 15m rule text says "TWAP of the time range", which could mean an average over all 15 minutes.
It does not. The Gamma API gives every window's `priceToBeat`. If the settle price is the TWAP
print at the close, each outcome equals `priceToBeat(next window) >= priceToBeat(this window)`.
That held in **120 of 120** consecutive resolved 15m windows (walked back from 2026-09-22,
`price_to_beat_chain.py 900 120 15m`). So the 15m market is close vs open on a 60 s smoothed
Chainlink series, and the close-vs-open maths below applies to it.

One consequence: the 1h direction is measured on Binance (the 1h market's source), but the
15m bet settles on Chainlink's 60 s TWAP. A 15m P(Up) has to be built on the Chainlink TWAP series.

## What the research says

**1. Reading (a) is Kelly's horse-race rule.** Betting a fraction of the stake on each outcome equal
to its probability ($b = p$) is the growth-optimal split when every dollar must be bet. That holds
whatever the odds (1901.06278, Prop. 2). At prices that sum to $1, growth is
$D(p_{true}\,\|\,price) - D(p_{true}\,\|\,b)$: the split makes money only when our probability is
closer to the true Up frequency than the price is, and splitting at the market's own ratio earns
exactly zero (2201.03387).

**2. At fair prices the split is the Kelly bet on one side plus cash.** With P(Up) = 0.80 and Up at
0.60, the 80/20 split leaves 1.3333× if Up wins and 0.5× if Down wins. The binary Kelly fraction
$f = (p - q)/(1 - q) = 0.50$ on Up, with the rest in cash, leaves exactly the same (`checks.py`;
2603.13581, 1201.6655, 2412.14144).

**3. When Up + Down cost more than $1, never hold both.** One Up plus one Down always redeems for $1
(2608.00666, 2604.24366). A taker pays both asks plus two fees, about $1.045 at 0.50/0.51. With cash
allowed, the log-optimal position is at most one side plus cash. Up is bought only when P(Up) is
above Up's all-in cost, and the stake is the Kelly fraction (2603.13581 Thm 3; 1901.06278 Thm 10;
the bid-ask version in 2607.06166). So "4 Up + 1 Down" is 3 net Up plus one pair that loses the
spread and both fees.

**4. Reading (b) is probability matching.** An 80/20 die picks the winning side 68% of the time,
against 80% for always taking the likelier side (2511.03643 restates the classic 62.5% vs 75% urn).
Its expected profit equals the split's, because expected profit is linear in the stakes, but its
log growth is lower by Jensen's inequality (1508.05241, eq. 1.7). Randomising is optimal only when
betting is the only way to learn a side's value (Thompson sampling: 1707.02038, 1205.4217), against
an adversary (1910.01913), or as a crowd equilibrium (2605.19440). Every 15m outcome is public
whatever we bet. In such full-information problems, taking the better side is optimal
(2302.04452, Cor. 2 and 5).

**5. Direction and volatility are one number.** Treat the window as a digital option: with forecast
drift $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$ over time $\tau$, $P(Up) \approx \Phi(\mu\sqrt{\tau}/\sigma)$
(2606.19517 gives the risk-neutral form; putting a forecast drift in its place is our extension).
For a fixed drift, higher volatility pulls P(Up) toward 50%. That shrinks the Kelly stake, which is
the maths version of "more mixing when volatility is high". At constant drift and volatility, 80% up
over the next hour is 66% up over the next 15 minutes. The drift is the weak input: one hour of data
estimates it with an error as large as a typical hourly move (2606.08209). The mapping from signal
to probability has to be fitted and checked for calibration (2008.03033, 0806.0813).

**6. What the data says about the inputs.**
- Over 15 minutes, crypto leans slightly toward reversal. Across 183 Binance pairs, 98% lean against
  the last 15m candle; BTC AUC is 0.533, about 52.3% accuracy (2608.21888).
- Trend payoff saturates for strong trends and, on futures 1h trends, turns to reversal beyond about
  1.8σ (2501.16772, 1404.3274).
- On Polymarket BTC 15m markets, the market's own mid (Brier 0.163) beat a 43-feature walk-forward
  model (0.165). A drift-only probability scored 0.218 (2607.26245). The bar a P(Up) must clear is
  the Up price, not 50%.
- Volatility follows the clock (peak around 16:00 UTC, bursts at :00/:15/:30/:45; 2109.12142,
  2607.09426). EWMA of recent realised volatility is a strong hourly baseline (1802.04065). 1h BTC
  returns have excess kurtosis near 30 (2010.07402), so a normal-curve P(Up) needs checking against
  outcomes.

**7. When P(Up) is uncertain.** Fractional Kelly equals full Kelly with the belief pulled toward the
market price, not toward 50% (1201.6655). Averaging the probability over parameter uncertainty beat
plug-in Kelly (1701.02814). A drawdown limit on a single bet gives a scaled-down Kelly bet
(1603.06183). Small sizing errors cost growth only quadratically (2412.14144).

## Papers (all 51, checked against arXiv)

### Kelly sizing and proportional betting
- **[1901.06278](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06278)** — Gambling and Rényi Divergence. Cédric Bleuler, Amos Lapidoth, Christoph Pfister (2019).
  - Result: Proposition 2: E[log S] = log c + D(p||r) − D(p||b), where c = 1/Σ(1/o_i) and r_i = c/o_i. So among fully invested bets, b = p is the unique optimum whatever the odds. For the utility (1/β)·log E[S^β]: with β ≥ 1 it is optimal to put everything on one horse (Prop. 3; β = 1 is plain expected return). With β < 1 the fully invested optimum bets on every horse. Proposition 9: if c ≥ 1 (fair or better odds), holding cash never helps, for any β ≠ 0. Theorem 10 is stated for β in (−∞,0)∪(0,1), a family that surrounds but excludes the log point β = 0. It says that if c < 1 (sub-fair odds), the optimal cash fraction is strictly positive and at least one horse gets no bet. Horse i is bet if and only if p_i·o_i > Γ = (1 − Σ_J p_i)/(1 − Σ_J 1/o_i), where J is the set of horses bet on. For the log case itself, the same rule is given in 2603.13581.
  - For this idea: This is reading (a). '80% up means 4 Up, 1 Down' is b = p. If all the money must be placed, b = p is the growth-best split whatever the prices (Prop. 2). If holding cash is allowed, b = p stays best only when Up + Down together cost $1 or less, fees included (Prop. 9). A taker buying both Polymarket sides pays more than $1 (ask + ask ≥ $1, plus fees), so c < 1. Theorem 10 then says hold some cash and buy at most one of the two sides, and only if probability × payout clears Γ. Whether c is above or below 1 depends on the prices actually paid.
- **[2603.13581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13581)** — Single-Event Multinomial Full Kelly via Implicit State Positions. Christopher D. Long (2026).
  - Result: With state prices q_i = 1/O_i (their sum may exceed 1), the canonical optimum is x_i = (p_i − c*·q_i)^+ (Theorem 3). An outcome is bet only if p_i/q_i > c*. The cash level c* = (1 − P_A)/(1 − Q_A) comes from one greedy pass over outcomes sorted by p_i/q_i, starting from c = 1. Optimal terminal wealth is max{c*, p_i/q_i}. Any genuinely active proper support must have Q_A < 1. In the binary case the stake is (p − q)/(1 − q), the classical Kelly fraction (Remark 4). The author presents this as a known result with a shorter proof.
  - For this idea: Gives the exact log-optimal stake for one 15m window when the fee-inclusive cost per share of Up and Down is used as q_Up and q_Down. Up is bet only if p_Up > q_Up. After adding Up, the cash level is p_Down/(1 − q_Up), and Down then qualifies only if q_Up + q_Down < 1. So when the two sides cost more than $1, the optimum is one side plus cash, or no bet at all, never both. At fair prices (q_Up + q_Down = 1), the paper's terminal-wealth formula shows the 80/20 split and 'Kelly fraction on Up plus cash' leave the same wealth: p/q_Up if Up wins, (1 − p)/q_Down if Down wins.
- **[1201.6655](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.6655)** — Learning Performance of Prediction Markets with Kelly Bettors. Alina Beygelzimer, John Langford, David Pennock (2012).
  - Result: The Kelly fraction for buying at price p_m is f* = (p − p_m)/(1 − p_m), and (p_m − p)/p_m for taking the other side. If all traders use Kelly, the equilibrium price is the wealth-weighted average belief, p_m = Σ w_i·p_i. Wealth is then redistributed by Bayes' rule, and the market's log loss satisfies L ≤ L_i + ln(1/w_i) for every trader i. λ-fractional Kelly is exactly full Kelly with the belief λp + (1 − λ)·p_m. In simulation with 100 full-Kelly agents, the price tracked the observed frequency and the wealth distribution matched a Beta posterior. The paper also notes that betting everything maximises expected profit but drives the chance of bankruptcy toward 1 over many bets.
  - For this idea: Gives the binary Kelly fraction for buying Up at price p_m when the 1h direction says probability p. The fractional-Kelly identity says the consistent way to hedge when unsure is to pull p toward the market price, not toward 50/50. Pulled all the way to the price, the bet is zero. The model assumes both sides trade at p_m and 1 − p_m, with no spread or fee.
- **[2201.03387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03387)** — Adaptive strategy in Kelly's horse races model. Armand Despons, David Lacoste, Luca Peliti (2022).
  - Result: With fair odds (inverse odds summing to 1) and all capital bet on every horse, long-run growth is D_KL(p||r) − D_KL(p||b). Capital grows only if the bettor's b is closer to the true p than the bookmaker's r, and b = r gives exactly zero growth. A bettor learning p from past results by Laplace's rule trails Kelly by an average regret ⟨Δ(t)⟩ = ⟨Δ(t0)⟩ + (M − 1)/2·log(t/t0 + 1), which does not depend on the odds. Its average log-capital falls for a learning phase of about t* = (M − 1)/(2·D_KL(p||r)) races, an asymptotic estimate the authors say overstates the real t*. Using the odds as the prior reduces the early loss when the odds are informative, but leaves the long-run regret and t* unchanged.
  - For this idea: States when the proportional split grows wealth at fair prices: your probability must be closer (in KL, measured from the true Up frequency) than the Up price is. Splitting at the market's own ratio earns nothing. For Up/Down (M = 2), the learner's regret is ½·log t and its log-capital bottoms out after about 1/(2·D(p||r)) windows. By that formula, if the true Up frequency is 0.55 against a fair 0.50 price (D ≈ 0.005 nats), a bettor learning that frequency from outcomes alone keeps losing for about 100 windows; break-even comes later. Limits: the model assumes fair prices, always bets both sides, and learns one fixed frequency rather than a signal-dependent probability like the 1h direction. In the paper's Markov extension, the learning cost grows with the number of conditioning states.
- **[2412.14144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14144)** — Application of the Kelly Criterion to Prediction Markets. Bernhard K. Meister (2024).
  - Result: For a contract at price p with belief q and no borrowing, the optimal fraction is f = (q − p)/(1 − p) = (Q − P)/(1 + Q), with P = p/(1−p) and Q = q/(1−q). With log-utility traders, clearing prices can differ widely from the capital-weighted mean belief (two-investor toy cases). In a double-or-nothing model with the stake held fixed, an error ε in the bias changes the KL rate for reaching a growth threshold linearly in ε. Mis-sizing around the optimal fraction 2p − 1 costs growth only quadratically: −ε²/(4p(1−p)). The paper explicitly ignores fees and the mirror contract.
  - For this idea: Confirms the Kelly fraction for one Polymarket Up share bought at price p with belief q, and that the price should not be read as the probability. Getting the stake slightly wrong around the optimum costs little expected growth, because the loss is quadratic. The first-order effect in the paper is on the chance of reaching a given growth target when the true bias differs from the assumed one. The linked Down contract and the taker fee are left out.
- **[2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166)** — When do prophets profit in prediction markets?. Anri Gu, Nicole Kagan, Alec Sun, Jibang Wu, Haifeng Xu (2026).
  - Result: For any strictly proper scoring rule with convex potential G, the bet s = ∇G(p) − ∇G(q) earns expected profit equal to the score gap S(p;p*) − S(q;p*), plus the Bregman divergence D_G(q,p), minus liquidity loss (Theorem 1). Theorem 2: any robustly profitable strategy is essentially this one. The Brier version stakes in proportion to p − q. On the standardized 200-event subset, Kelly (levered when the Kelly ratio exceeded 1) returned −99.9% ROI for 4 of 5 AI forecasters, while the Brier proper bet returned +22.1% for the most accurate one (Claude Opus 4.6). This backtest assumed zero price impact. The bid-ask extension buys YES only if p > the YES ask, buys NO only if p < 1 − the NO ask, and otherwise does not bet. In a 26-day live Kalshi run, a Gemini 3 agent grew $200 to $360.67 (+80.33% ROI, Sharpe 3.35 annualised) with 236 filled orders across 129 markets and $26.31 in fees.
  - For this idea: Addresses sizing each window from the gap between the 1h-direction probability and the price: stakes grow with p − q. The bid-ask rule gives Up/Down directly: buy Up only if p is above the Up ask, buy Down only if p is below 1 − the Down ask, otherwise no bet, so never both sides. Full Kelly was fragile when p was miscalibrated, though their Kelly baseline also used leverage. The decomposition shows profit can come from differing from the price (the Bregman term) as well as from being more accurate.
- **[1812.10371](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.10371)** — Distributional Robust Kelly Gambling: Optimal Strategy under Uncertainty in the Long-Run. Qingyun Sun, Stephen Boyd (2018).
  - Result: The robust Kelly problem is convex but not tractable in general. It has tractable forms solvable with standard convex tools for polyhedral, box, ellipsoidal (p-norm), f-divergence (including KL), Wasserstein, and estimated mean/covariance uncertainty sets. The robust strategy asymptotically maximises the worst-case growth rate and dominates any essentially different strategy, extending Breiman. In a 20-horse place-betting example (190 outcomes, parimutuel returns), nominal Kelly grew 4.3% under the nominal distribution but −2.2% in the worst case. Robust Kelly grew 2.2% nominally and, in the worst case, 0.7% (box, η = 0.26) or 0.4% (ball, c = 0.016).
  - For this idea: Formalises 'I'm not sure the probability is 80%': put a box or KL ball around the 1h-direction probability and maximise worst-case growth. For one binary contract with cash, growth is linear in the probability. So the robust bet amounts to sizing from the least favourable probability in the set, and to no bet if that set contains the price. This reduction is our reading of the maths, not a result stated in the paper. It trades some expected growth for protection if p is wrong.
- **[1701.02814](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.02814)** — Kelly betting on horse races with uncertainty in probability estimates. Michael R. Metel (2017).
  - Result: Parameters are modelled as β ~ N(β̂, Σ), with a sandwich covariance from multinomial logistic regression. Four simulated experiments of 2,500 trials each were run: two outcomes at identical odds 1.1 and 1.2, 10 outcomes at odds 2, and 30 outcomes at odds 4. Scoring used total expected log return under the true probabilities. True probabilities scored 27.463 and plug-in Kelly 18.134. Half Kelly scored 13.268 and Monte-Carlo-averaged probabilities 18.471. Expected-log with a chance constraint (ECCx, α = 0.4) was best at 18.484. Averaged probabilities and ECCx at α = 0.4 beat plug-in Kelly in all four experiments. Tighter chance constraints lowered growth: the pure chance-constrained model scored 12.158 at α = 0.1, and ECCx scored 14.599 at α = 0.1. The two-outcome chance-constrained problem (CC2) is solved exactly.
  - For this idea: Directly models the case where the 1h-direction probability comes from a fitted model with estimation error, and has an exact two-outcome form that fits Up/Down. The two-outcome experiments use heavily over-round odds with cash allowed, like a taker's view of Up/Down. Averaging the probability over parameter uncertainty beat plug-in Kelly. Flat half-Kelly lost more growth than plug-in Kelly and every model except the tightest chance constraint.
- **[2107.08827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08827)** — Optimal sports betting strategies in practice: an experimental review. Matej Uhrín, Gustav Šourek, Ondřej Hubáček, Filip Železný (2021).
  - Result: Data: 2,700 horse races (20% margin, A_KL ≈ 0.0022), 16,000 NBA games (two outcomes, 3.8% margin, A_KL ≈ −0.0146) and 32,000 football matches (3% margin, A_KL ≈ −0.013). Without a KL advantage (NBA, football), full Kelly was ruined in 100% of runs. Fractional Kelly, with its fraction grid-searched on a training split, had 0% ruin and median final wealth of 2.4 (NBA) and 10.05 (football). In horse racing, full Kelly reached median 4.11 with 0.6% ruin, and fractional Kelly 3.39 with 0% ruin. Drawdown-constrained Kelly performed close to fractional Kelly. Robust Kelly was the most stable but generally ended with the lowest wealth among the risk-controlled strategies.
  - For this idea: Shows empirically how Kelly-type sizing behaves when probabilities come from a model, including a two-outcome market like Up/Down. Full Kelly's fate followed the KL advantage over the price: it was ruined whenever the model was less accurate than the bookmaker. Tuned fractional Kelly avoided ruin and still ended with median gains even with a negative KL advantage. The authors warn that this does not hold universally.
- **[1603.06183](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06183)** — Risk-Constrained Kelly Gambling. Enzo Busseti, Ernest K. Ryu, Stephen Boyd (2016).
  - Result: The constraint Prob(W_min < α) < β is replaced by the convex sufficient condition E[(rᵀb)^−λ] ≤ 1, with λ = log β / log α. For two outcomes, if the Kelly bet breaks the constraint, the constrained bet is a fractional Kelly bet. In an example with 19 risky bets plus cash and 100 outcomes, Kelly grew at 0.062 with a 39.7% chance of a drawdown beyond 30%. The constrained bet (λ = 6.456) grew at 0.043 with 7.3% drawdown risk. At about 10% risk, fractional Kelly grew at about 0.035, versus 0.047 for the constrained bet (λ = 5.5). In a second example with lognormal returns, fractional Kelly performed essentially the same as the constrained bet.
  - For this idea: For a single Up-or-Down position per window, the best drawdown-limited bet is exactly a scaled-down Kelly bet. That gives a formula for picking the fraction from a tolerated drawdown (α, β). With several markets at once, the constrained bet beat uniform scaling by a wide margin in their finite-outcome example, but matched it in their lognormal example.

### Probability matching and randomised bets
- **[2511.03643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03643)** — Explaining Human Choice Probabilities with Simple Vector Representations. Peter A. V. DiBerardino, Britt Anderson (2025).
  - Result: Restates the classic cost of matching using Koehler and James's (2009) urn of 30 green and 10 red marbles: always picking the likelier colour wins 75% of the time, while matching wins 0.75^2 + 0.25^2 = 62.5%. In three hide-and-seek experiments (281 students in total: 50 in the lab, 54 online, 177 in Experiment 3; 2 and 5 rooms, then 3, 5 and 7 rooms), each person's seeking frequencies were almost exactly a mix of maximising and matching (points lie on alpha + beta = 1). Hiding frequencies were a mix of minimising and 'antimatching', which reflects the opponent's distribution across the uniform one. The authors call matching 'exploration' (learning what to expect) and maximising 'exploitation'. In Experiments 1 and 3 (less so in 2), people were closer to optimal when hiding than when seeking, which the authors link to prospect theory.
  - For this idea: Reading (b), rolling an 80/20 die each window, wins 0.8^2 + 0.2^2 = 68% of the time against 80% for always taking the 1h side. This is the same sum as the paper's 62.5% vs 75%. The paper treats matching as exploration but never says that learning is its only justification. The next step is our inference: that rationale adds nothing on 15m markets, because every window's outcome is public whichever side you bet. In the urn task the paper cites, draws were visible too, so matching there is the documented bias, not a learning strategy.
- **[q-bio/0510007](https://arxiv.org/abs/q-bio/0510007)** — The fitness value of information. Carl T. Bergstrom, Michael Lachmann (2005).
  - Result: The model has two states with probabilities p1 and p2, and a stake earns w_i only if it matches the state. One-period expected payoff is maximised by putting everything on the larger p_i w_i, for a value of max[p1 w1, p2 w2]. But backing one outcome every period eventually meets a zero-payoff period and wipes out. Long-run log growth is maximised by x = p1, whatever w1 and w2 are (bet-hedging). With partial payoffs (1, b; a, 1), hedging is optimal only in a middle band of p; outside that band everything goes on one side. In the all-or-nothing model, a perfectly informative cue adds growth exactly equal to its mutual information with the state, and the payoffs drop out. In general the gain equals I(E;C) only when every option is held both with and without the cue, and is lower otherwise. A probability-matching mixture of the cue-conditional strategies loses at most H(C), so the value of information is bounded by the mutual information.
  - For this idea: This is the maths behind reading (a). An 80/20 split across Up and Down is the growth-optimal split when the whole bankroll must be committed every window, and the paper shows x = p then holds whatever the payouts. The 1h signal can add at most its mutual information with the 15m result to the growth rate. The paper does not model a cash option. Standard Kelly theory (not this paper) covers that case: when cash is allowed and Up plus Down costs more than $1, holding one share of each is worse than holding cash, so the growth-optimal position holds at most one side, sized by its edge.
- 2201.03387: see Kelly sizing and proportional betting above.
- **[2302.04452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04452)** — An Information-Theoretic Analysis of Nonstationary Bandit Learning. Seungki Min, Daniel J. Russo (2023).
  - Result: Theorem 1: for any algorithm, per-period regret is at most sqrt(Gamma * H(A*)), where H(A*) is the entropy rate of the best-action sequence. Corollary 1: for Thompson sampling this is at most sigma * sqrt(2 |A| H(A*)). Corollary 2: in full-information problems, Greedy <= Thompson sampling <= sigma * sqrt(2 H(A*)). Corollary 5 says that in full-information problems 'the optimal policy is the rule' Greedy. In terms of the effective horizon (the mean time between switches of the best action), Thompson sampling's regret is O~(sigma * sqrt(|A| / tau_eff)), and Proposition 3 builds an instance where no algorithm does better, up to log factors. For fast-changing environments they propose satisficing variants that explore less.
  - For this idea: A 15m Up/Down window is a full-information problem: both sides settle publicly whatever you bet. By Corollary 2, taking the side with the larger expected payoff has regret no higher than Thompson sampling in such problems, and Corollary 5 calls that greedy rule optimal there. How often the better side flips limits how well any rule can do, because regret is bounded by the entropy rate of the best-side sequence. Linking that flip rate to volatility is our inference. Neither result is a reason to randomise.
- **[1707.02038](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02038)** — A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling. Daniel J. Russo, Benjamin Van Roy, Abbas Kazerouni, Ian Osband, Zheng Wen (2017).
  - Result: Thompson sampling, 'also known as posterior sampling and probability matching', picks each action with the posterior probability that it is the optimal one. The tutorial stresses that it samples a plausible success probability, not a plausible outcome. For Bernoulli bandits its regret matches the Lai-Robbins asymptotic lower bound, and Agrawal and Goyal give a worst-case bound of O(sqrt(K T log T)). Example 8.1: for any stationary deterministic rule there is a prior under which regret grows linearly, so a stationary rule must randomise to learn, although deterministic non-stationary rules such as UCB also work. Section 8.2.1: Thompson sampling is a poor choice when learning needs no active exploration and is usually beaten by greedier rules; the example given is portfolio selection. Section 8.2.3: it can also do poorly on time-sensitive problems, where exploiting a good but suboptimal action beats exploring.
  - For this idea: This is the formal version of the 'smart dice roll', with one correction: roll by the probability that each side is the better bet at its price, not by the probability that it wins. The tutorial justifies randomising only as a way to learn when an action's value is revealed only by taking it. Up/Down outcomes are public, so by the tutorial's own portfolio example a greedier rule should do better.
- **[2605.19440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19440)** — Competitive Search with a Faulty Satnav (GPS): When Probability Matching is Rational. Steve Alpern, Mark Broom (2026).
  - Result: n searchers on a star graph with k wrong leaves race to a prize, which is split among the first to arrive. A shared pointer is right with probability p, and each searcher follows it with probability q. For every n, k and p there is a unique symmetric equilibrium q-bar. It rises with p, rises with k, and eventually falls with n (Theorem 3). For finite n it is above p (Theorem 2), and it tends to p as n goes to infinity, which is the case where probability matching is rational. With many searchers, a focal player's payoff is about (1/n)[p r / q + (1-p)(1-r)/(1-q)]. The best response is to always follow (r = 1) if the crowd's q < p, never follow (r = 0) if q > p, and anything is equally good at q = p. For n = 5 and k = 3, p = 1/2, 2/3 and 3/4 give q-bar = 0.53, 0.70 and 0.78.
  - For this idea: By analogy, the large-crowd payoff looks like a pari-mutuel price: the crowd share q plays the role of the Up price, and p/q is the payout per unit. So matching appears only as the crowd's equilibrium, where price equals probability and nobody has an edge. The same best-response logic says a single trader facing an Up price below p should put the whole bet on Up rather than mix.
